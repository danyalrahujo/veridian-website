Why Most Enterprise AI Pilots Never Reach Production

Priya Nathan, VP Data EngineeringMarch 4, 20267 min read

Almost every enterprise we talk to has run at least one AI pilot that impressed everyone in the room and then quietly disappeared. The pattern is consistent enough that it's worth naming directly: the model was rarely the problem. What stalled the project was everything around it.

The gap between a demo and a production system

A demo needs to work once, on a curated dataset, in front of a friendly audience. A production system needs to work every time, on messy live data, under latency constraints, with a defined answer for what happens when it's wrong. Teams that treat the demo as 90% of the work consistently underestimate the remaining 10% — which is usually closer to half the total effort.

Three gaps that stall almost every pilot

First, there's usually no evaluation harness — no systematic way to say whether a new model version is actually better than the last one, beyond someone's subjective read of a handful of examples. Second, ownership becomes unclear the moment legal, security, or compliance ask a question the pilot team can't answer. Third, monitoring is an afterthought, so nobody notices when the model's performance quietly degrades three months after launch.

What changes the outcome

The engagements that make it to production consistently build the evaluation harness before the model, assign a named business owner before the first line of code, and treat monitoring as a launch requirement rather than a nice-to-have. None of this is exotic — it's closer to standard software engineering discipline than it is to advanced AI research. That's also why it's so often skipped: it isn't the interesting part.

If your organization has a pilot that's stalled, the fastest diagnostic question is rarely "is the model good enough?" It's usually "who owns this once it's live, and how would we know if it broke?" If neither has a clear answer, that's the actual blocker — not the model.
